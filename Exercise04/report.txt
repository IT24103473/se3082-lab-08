Exercise 4 - Time and Speedup vs Number of Processes
====================================================

The sheet says "for both Exercise 1 and Exercise 2", but Exercise 1 is the GitHub repo
and has nothing to time. The two parallel programs are Exercise 2 (sum) and
Exercise 3 (Monte Carlo Pi), so those two are measured here.

Files
  run_timings.sh       runs sum_mpi and pi_mpi with 1..12 processes, 7 runs each
  timings.csv          every one of the 168 measured times
  plot_timings.py      draws the graphs from timings.csv (needs matplotlib)
  tables.md            the tables below, printed by plot_timings.py
  sum_mpi_time.png     Exercise 2: Time vs number of processes
  sum_mpi_speedup.png  Exercise 2: Speedup vs number of processes
  pi_mpi_time.png      Exercise 3: Time vs number of processes
  pi_mpi_speedup.png   Exercise 3: Speedup vs number of processes

How it was measured
  * Machine: laptop with an Intel Core i5-1235U (12 hardware threads), WSL Ubuntu
    24.04, Open MPI 4.1.6, gcc 13.3. mpirun --use-hwthread-cpus lets MPI use all
    12 threads.
  * The time is MPI_Wtime on rank 0, from after an MPI_Barrier until the last result
    has been received. Starting mpirun is not included.
  * Each case was run 7 times. The graphs use the MEDIAN, and the shaded band on the
    time graphs shows the fastest and slowest run.
  * Speedup S(p) = T(1) / T(p).  Efficiency = S(p) / p.

What the graphs show
  1. Both programs get faster with more processes, but the speedup is far below the
     ideal straight line, and it stops improving at about 4.5x after 8-10 processes.

  2. Pi scales almost perfectly from 1 to 2 processes (1.91x), then much less well.
     The main reason is the processor. The i5-1235U is a hybrid CPU: it has 2 fast
     "performance" cores (each with 2 hardware threads) and 8 slower "efficiency"
     cores. With 2 processes, both can run on the fast cores. With more processes,
     some land on slower cores or share a core through hyper-threading. The work is
     split into EQUAL parts, so rank 0 has to wait for the slowest process, and the
     slow processes decide the total time.
     A laptop also runs one core at a higher turbo clock than it can run all cores at
     once, so each core gets a little slower as more of them are busy.

  3. The sum (Exercise 2) is much noisier: look at the wide bands and the zig-zag
     speedup line. The whole job takes only about 9 ms on one process, so with many
     processes each one works for less than 1 ms. At that scale, fixed costs (the
     barrier, sending the partial sums, the operating system scheduling the
     processes) are a large part of the measured time, and small disturbances
     change the result a lot. The peak at 6 processes (4.23x) and the dip at 7 are
     noise, not a real effect.

  4. The Pi program has much more work per process (about 250 ms in total), so the
     fixed costs matter less and its curves are smoother. This is the general rule:
     parallelism pays off when each process has a lot of work compared with the
     cost of communicating.

  5. Past 10 processes the time no longer improves (Pi: 0.057 s at 10 and 0.058 s at
     12), because there are no more free cores to add, only hardware threads that
     share a core with another process.
